# Voice

How the copy is written. Styling alone does not make something read as GeneralMind; the sentences do half the work. The examples below are written for a made-up company in the same voice. Write new copy for the subject at hand; do not lift sentences from generalmind.com.

Source note: these rules come from the website, case studies and blog articles. Their LinkedIn posts sit behind a login and were not read, so the social-post section is derived from the article voice. If the user pastes real posts, match those first.

## Who is speaking

Someone who has run the operation they are describing. They know which supplier sends scanned PDFs and which portal has to be checked on Thursdays. They are calm, exact and a little dry. They respect the reader's time and intelligence, they do not sell, and they will say when their product is the wrong choice. The reader is an operations, finance or IT lead at a large company who has heard every AI pitch already.

## Seven habits

1. **Name the work, not the vision.** Say what gets read, matched, chased, booked. "AI matches the confirmation to the order, flags a changed price, and updates the record" - not "AI transforms procurement".
2. **Short declaratives.** Most sentences are under 20 words. One idea each. A long sentence is allowed when it is a list of concrete things.
3. **Contrast instead of adjectives.** The typical move is "It is not X. It is Y." or "A tool that reads a photo and then asks a clerk to type the number has saved nobody any time." The contrast carries the emphasis that an adjective would fake.
4. **Give the number, the unit and the period.** "94% of readings booked with zero edits", "about 1,100 lines a week", "within eight weeks". Never "significantly faster".
5. **Keep people in the sentence.** The AI does the routine; a person decides the uncertain. Say both halves: "Routine runs itself. Every uncertain call goes to your team."
6. **Say where it does not apply.** Every substantial piece has one honest limit: who should not buy, what has to be true first, what the pilot will not prove. This is what makes the rest believable.
7. **End on the plain outcome.** Close a section with what is now different for the person doing the job, not with a slogan.

## Words

Use: reads, extracts, matches, checks, flags, routes, escalates, chases, follows up, reconciles, books, posts, updates, drafts, sends · order, invoice, confirmation, receipt, exception, threshold, confidence score, audit trail, master data, record, queue, hand-off, pilot · today, by hand, on your own data, side by side, without touching your ERP, no commitment.

Avoid: revolutionary, game-changing, cutting-edge, next-generation, seamless, effortless, unlock, unleash, supercharge, empower, elevate, leverage, harness, transform (as a promise), reimagine, magic, delight, world-class, best-in-class, robust, holistic, synergy, journey (for a product flow, though "order journey" as a term of trade is fine), "in today's fast-paced world", "we're excited to", "let's dive in".

- "AI" is a plain noun doing a job: "AI drafts the request and follows up." Not "our intelligent AI-powered solution".
- GeneralMind's automated mode is a proper noun: Autopilot (AutoPilot inside the product UI). For another company, use its own name for that mode if it has one; otherwise "autopilot" as a common word, or plain words ("runs without review").
- "We" is the company, "you" is the reader, "your team" are the people who stay in control.
- Light wordplay is allowed only in names of things (a blog called after the company name, a category title). Never in body copy or headlines.

## Punctuation and casing

- Sentence case on pages, decks and documents: headlines, buttons, labels. Capitalise proper nouns and named use cases ("PO Confirmation", "Autopilot"). Inside the product UI, tabs, filters and row or primary actions are short Title Case labels ("All Suppliers", "More Filters", "View PO Lines"); everything else there is sentence case.
- A headline that is a sentence ends with a full stop. A headline that is a label does not.
- No exclamation marks. No emoji. No ellipses for suspense.
- Colons introduce: "The gap: …", "Three things make it worse each year:". Semicolons are fine in long-form.
- For an aside, a spaced hyphen " - " or a colon. The em dash is rare; do not lean on it.
- Serial comma in marketing lists ("emails, decisions, and exceptions").
- British spelling in English long-form (behaviour, normalised, programme) unless the user's audience is American.
- Curly quotes and apostrophes (“ ” ’). Arrows are typographic: → for next, ↗ for external.
- Slashes join paired terms: "PO Confirmation / Follow-up", "Results / Impact".

## Numbers

- Numerals for everything measurable, including small counts in data contexts ("6 weeks", "3 systems"). Words for casual counts in prose ("three pressures").
- Ranges with "to": "10x to 50x", "30 to 50 cases", "93 to 95%".
- Approximation is stated, not hidden: "around 85%", "about 60%", "roughly 2,500 orders a month", "~800 employees".
- A percentage always says of what: "64% of lines booked with zero edits".
- Money with symbol and scale: `$18M`, `€6.4 billion`, `EUR 198 000` in tables.
- Dates: `Sep 3, 2026` in meta, `15 Sept 2026 14:30` in product tables, `Q3 2025` in timelines.
- Big-number labels are lower-case fragments: "total raised", "of lines booked with zero edits", "PO lines validated / week".
- Never invent a metric. If the user did not give the number, write the sentence without it. A figure you derive from their facts with a stated assumption is allowed when it is marked: "(est.)" after its label, "(target)" for a goal, and one line saying what the estimate assumes. `~` means the source itself was approximate, not that you guessed.

## Patterns by element

**Eyebrow** - two to four words, uppercase, naming what this section does for the reader. A stage direction, not a slogan: `HOW IT FITS`, `SEE IT RUN`, `BEFORE YOU DECIDE`, `45-MINUTE WALKTHROUGH`, `RESULTS AT <CUSTOMER>`.

**Headline** - pick one of these shapes:
- Promise, then control (hero): "Every meter reading, handled. Every exception, yours to decide."
- Named problem: "The gap: the work between your inbox and your billing system"
- Two imperatives: "Begin with one process. Add the next when it has earned it."
- Plain claim: "Desks that already run this way."
- Plain label: "Works with what you run" · "What people ask first"
- Beats: "Read. Check. Book. Autopilot."
- Question, then answer: "Not covered here? Write to us."

**Lead** - one or two sentences. Often a list of concrete verbs: "Our AI reads move-in forms, matches meter readings, corrects billing runs and answers supplier switches, then updates your billing system."

**Feature item** - title of two to four words, then one sentence under 15 words that states the mechanism and who benefits: "Priced per closed request. You pay when a request is closed, not per seat."

**Numbered step** - "1. Reads and sorts" then one sentence beginning with the actor: "Our AI reads what arrives by email, photo and portal, and sorts readings from everything else."

**Accordion item (capability)** - a noun phrase as the title; body starts with "AI" and a chain of verbs, and may end by naming what disappears: "AI takes readings from photos, emails and the portal, tests them against the history, books the plausible ones and routes the rest to a clerk. No more keying from photos."

**FAQ answer** - the answer in the first word or clause ("No.", "Yes.", "The pilot is free."), then the specifics, then one sentence of guarantee: "Below your threshold, a clerk decides."

**Button** - verb plus plain noun, two or three words: "Try for free", "Read case study", "Open the trust centre", "Send request".

**Callout** - one sentence stating the consequence: "So the process is slowest in the six weeks that matter."

**Table capability row** - plain statement the reader can verify: "Learns from each correction", "A person decides every uncertain case".

## Patterns by format

**Landing page** - hero (promise + control) → the problem named → what the AI does, as a list of capabilities → proof (cases, quote) → how it works in numbered steps → how to start (one use case, free pilot) → integrations → security → comparison → FAQ → contact. Each section answers the question the previous one raised.

**Case study** - fixed headings, in this order: About <Customer> · Use-case definition · The challenge (a one-line setup ending in a colon, three named pillars, then a callout stating the consequence) · The solution (say that the product works on top of the systems already in place; a three-step flow; a callout about what people still decide) · Results (first the big numbers, then two-to-three-word chips for what changed in practice) · the customer quote. Title: "How <Customer> <did the concrete thing>." - past tense, the customer as the subject, a number if there is one.

**Customer POV / one-pager** - title is the outcome with a time frame ("<Customer>’s <process>, on Autopilot in eight weeks."). Then: what we see today (three observations, each a fact about their operation) · what it costs (three numbers) · what we would do (three dated steps) · what you get (three numbers) · the next step (free, small, specific). Mark every estimate as an estimate and say what it is based on.

**Deck** - one sentence per slide headline, and it must make sense read alone. Slide titles are claims ("Every decision is scored before anything is booked."), not topics ("Scoring"). Body text is a lead or three points, never paragraphs.

**Article** - title in sentence case, stating what the reader will know. A one-paragraph lede that is the whole argument. "Key takeaways": four or five full sentences, one of which says when not to buy. H2s are plain clauses ("Where the eleven minutes go", "When you should not buy this", "What the team does instead"). Paragraphs of three to five sentences with a concrete example in each. Figures are captioned with a sentence fragment that states the point. Close with an FAQ of three to five direct answers.

**Social post (derived)** -
- Line 1 is a plain claim or a specific observation, under 15 words, no hook formula, no emoji.
- Then two to four short paragraphs of one to three sentences: what the work really is, a concrete detail, what changes.
- One number with its unit and period.
- One honest limit or condition.
- Last line: a quiet next step ("The case study is in the comments." / "We run a free pilot on one process.").
- No hashtag walls (zero to three, at the end), no "Thoughts?", no bullet lists of emoji, no line-per-sentence staccato.
- The image is a cover (`cover.html`) whose title repeats the first line.

**Email** - subject is the specific outcome ("Your move-in desk: what we saw on Tuesday"). Three short paragraphs: what we understood, what we suggest, the one next step with a date. Plain text styling; see `formats.md`.

## German

The same voice in German: plain, concrete, "Sie" for enterprise readers. Short main clauses, verbs early, no anglicism pile-ups ("KI liest die Auftragsbestätigung, gleicht sie mit der Bestellung ab und meldet Abweichungen."). Compound nouns over marketing English. Sentence case follows normal German capitalisation. Numbers in German format (1.100 Zeilen pro Woche, 94 %).
